# Oncologist Cognitive Burden / Decision Fatigue in Manual Multi-Omics Synthesis

## Are there published surveys or qualitative studies on oncologist attitudes toward interpreting NGS/comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) reports — confidence, time burden, VUS difficulty?

### Takeaway
Multiple peer-reviewed surveys (2017–2021) consistently show oncologists have moderate-to-low confidence interpreting genomic reports — confidence drops sharply as test complexity rises (single-gene → panel → WES/WGS) and for secondary/germline findings — and a majority explicitly want interpretive assistance. A dedicated VUS-focused physician survey found accuracy as low as ~15% on realistic case vignettes.

### Cited Findings
- In a national US survey of genomic test users, >75% were "moderately" or "very" confident using multimarker tumor panel test results to guide care, but oncologists were "most confident" with single-gene tests and "least confident" using whole-genome/whole-exome sequencing to guide care; confidence was also significantly lower for discussing secondary/incidental germline findings than for consenting or discussing CGP results generally — [Oncologist Confidence in Genomic Testing and Implications for Using Multimarker Tumor Panel Tests in Practice, JCO Precision Oncology, 2020](https://ascopubs.org/doi/full/10.1200/PO.19.00338)
- Australian nationwide survey of medical oncologists (n=108 respondents, 32% response rate, published Oncotarget, Oct 2021): median self-rated confidence (0–100 VAS) was 75 for consenting patients to CGP referral, 70 for discussing CGP results, but only 56 for discussing secondary germline findings (p<0.001 vs. other tasks); 62.9% (68/108) "reported they needed, or wanted, assistance with interpreting CGP results"; confidence in recommending unproven/off-label therapy rose ~4.8 VAS points per 5 years of clinical experience (p=0.001) — [The experiences and needs of Australian medical oncologists in integrating comprehensive genomic profiling into clinical care: a nation-wide survey, Oncotarget, 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8522847/)
- In pediatric oncology, only 35% of surveyed physicians were confident interpreting/using/discussing somatic genomic test results, and only 27% were confident doing so for germline results — [Integrating next-generation sequencing into pediatric oncology practice: physician confidence and understanding of clinical genomics, Cancer, 2017 (Johnson et al.)](https://acsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cncr.30581)
- In a Mayo Clinic physician survey (84–92 respondents across specialties, fielded 2017) testing interpretation of variants of uncertain significance (VUS) via case vignettes: only 13/84 (14.6%) of physicians answered all three case examples with the literature-supported "most correct" answer; nearly half (39/84) incorrectly defined what a VUS is; over 75% made an unwarranted genetic-testing recommendation; cancer-specialist physicians did significantly better than non-specialists (P=.02) but still fell well short of full accuracy — [Physician interpretation of variants of uncertain significance, Familial Cancer, 2018 (Macklin et al.)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29721668/)
- A 2018 industry survey (Cardinal Health, >160 oncologists) found nearly 60% of oncologists cited difficulty interpreting test results as a key reason they do not use genomic tests more, attributed in part to the number of different tests, conflicting results across vendors, and oncologists' limited experience reading reports — [Barriers to genomic testing get in the way of precision medicine, STAT News, June 20, 2018](https://www.statnews.com/2018/06/20/genomic-testing-barriers-precision-medicine/)
- A structured-approach paper on commercial genomic reports notes that even among well-resourced expert panels reviewing identical datasets, interpretive concordance is incomplete — independent panels reached full/partial agreement in only ~79% of cases — underscoring that interpretation difficulty is not just a training gap but an inherent complexity problem — [Making sense of commercial genomic reports in oncology: a structured approach, Frontiers in Oncology, 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oncology/articles/10.3389/fonc.2026.1864818/full)

### Inferences
- Confidence scales inversely with data complexity/breadth (single gene → panel → WES/WGS; somatic → germline), which is exactly the storyboard's "fragmented multi-omics" scenario — pain is worst precisely where an integrated tool would add the most value.
- The consistent "want/need assistance" finding (Australia: 62.9%; STAT/Cardinal Health: ~60% cite interpretation difficulty) gives two independently sourced, similar-magnitude statistics from different countries/years that can be cited together for robustness.

## What published evidence exists on molecular tumor board (MTB) workload, turnaround time, and human effort per case?

### Takeaway
MTB case-preparation is a measurable, non-trivial time cost per case (roughly 30–65 minutes of manual prep depending on tumor type and role), and turnaround from genomic result to actionable recommendation has historically run into weeks, well past guideline targets — both figures are exactly the kind of "hours of human effort per case" evidence the storyboard needs.

### Cited Findings
- Before digital tumor-board tooling, average case preparation time was 65 minutes/case for a breast cancer tumor board, 42.6 minutes/case for a GI cancer tumor board, and 52.4 minutes/case for an ENT cancer tumor board (59, 61, and 36 cases respectively, 2020 cross-sectional analysis); after implementing a digital tumor board solution (NAVIFY Tumor Board), preparation time fell 28% (to 46.7 min), 23% (to 32.7 min), and 33% (to 35 min) respectively — an overall ~30% reduction in preparation time across the three boards, with nurse navigators seeing the largest individual improvement (69% reduction) — [Digital Tumor Board Solutions Have Significant Impact on Case Preparation, JCO Clinical Cancer Informatics, 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7469605/)
- International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer (IASLC) consensus recommends MTB meetings be completed within 1 hour (flexibility to 2 hours for complex cases), and that no more than 14 days should elapse between molecular results becoming available and multidisciplinary discussion — implying that even with a target, weeks can pass between report and decision without dedicated infrastructure — [Molecular Tumor Boards: A Consensus Statement From the International Association for the Study of Lung Cancer, Journal of Thoracic Oncology, 2025](https://www.jto.org/article/S1556-0864(25)00831-7/fulltext)
- In real-world (non-optimized) settings, mean turnaround time from genomic profiling request to MTB recommendation has been reported at 38.4 days (range 12.4–86 days) — found via aggregated search of MTB turnaround-time literature; the exact primary source article was not individually confirmed/fetched in this pass, so the report writer should treat this figure as needing primary-source verification before quoting it as a hard statistic. By contrast, streamlined/optimized workflows have hit 100% of NGS reports meeting a 72-hour turnaround target, and one virtual community-practice MTB reported average turnaround from referral to interpretation of under 10 hours — illustrating just how wide the gap is between unsupported manual review and tooled/optimized workflows.

### Inferences
- The 30–65 minutes of dedicated human prep time per case (pre-digital-tooling) is a strong, citable proxy for "hours of expert effort per patient" that a storyboard can dramatize — especially since this is prep time alone, not the actual board discussion, chart review, or literature lookup an individual oncologist does independently before that meeting.
- The magnitude of turnaround-time variance (single-digit hours to ~86 days depending on infrastructure) visually supports a "before/after" narrative arc for the pitch (fragmented manual process vs. integrated computational tool).

### Gaps
- The specific primary source for the "38.4 days mean, 12.4–86 day range" turnaround-time statistic was not individually verified/fetched (it surfaced only in an aggregated web-search summary spanning several MTB turnaround-time papers). Recommend the report writer/storyboard team verify this figure against a specific paper (candidates from the search: Nature Cancer's "Molecular Tumor Board Portal," PMC10123678, or PMC7446498 "Personalized Clinical Decision Making Through Implementation of a Molecular Tumor Board: A German Single-Center Experience") before using it as a standalone citation.
- Could not find a study that isolates and quantifies an individual oncologist's own independent review time (as distinct from institutional MTB team prep time) for a single genomic report — this remains a gap; the closest available proxy is the per-case MTB preparation-time data above.

## Is there literature on "information overload" in precision oncology — the gap between genomic data volume and clinicians' capacity/training to act on it?

### Takeaway
This is a well-established and explicitly named theme in the precision-oncology literature (sometimes termed a "data tsunami" or "interpretation gap"), with multiple 2022–2025 review/commentary papers stating plainly that data volume has outpaced clinicians' practical capacity to interpret it manually, motivating AI/computational decision-support tools.

### Cited Findings
- "Clinicians may face an overload of information, increasing the complexity of decision-making and the risk of non-optimal choices in precision oncology" — oncology requires clinicians to evaluate "a huge amount of data from diverse sources including genomic reports, imaging scans, pathology results, and electronic health records," an "enormous volume of information" that "can be overwhelming and time-consuming, leading to slower decision making" — [AI-driven multi-omics integration in precision oncology: bridging the data deluge to clinical decisions, Clinical and Experimental Medicine, 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10238-025-01965-9)
- "The complex nature of genomic information presents a huge challenge for clinicians in interpreting the patient's genomic alterations and selecting the optimum approved or investigational therapy," compounded because "collecting comprehensive information on genomic variants is difficult since the underlying data is dispersed over a multitude of distributed, heterogeneous, sometimes conflicting, and quickly evolving data sources" — [Precision oncology platforms: practical strategies for genomic database utilization in cancer treatment, Molecular Cytogenetics, 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13039-024-00698-w)
- A paper explicitly titled around the "data tsunami" concept frames the core tension as whether the current volume of multi-omics data can actually "guide rational treatment decisions" in practice, versus simply accumulating faster than it can be interpreted — [Precision medicine in the era of multi-omics: can the data tsunami guide rational treatment decision?, ESMO Open, 2023](https://www.esmoopen.com/article/S2059-7029(23)00877-3/fulltext) (full text was not directly accessible — 403 Forbidden — so this entry relies on the title/framing found via search rather than a confirmed pull-quote; report writer should attempt independent access before quoting specifics from the body text).
- Clinicians' manual strategy for finding the clinical meaning of genomic variants "requires time, is subject to omission of information, and depends on the clinician's ability to interpret clinical implications from genomic research articles" — surfaced via aggregated search of precision-oncology data-complexity literature (exact originating paper not individually confirmed in this pass; treat as thematically representative rather than a pinned citation until verified).

### Inferences
- The "data tsunami" / "data deluge" framing recurring independently across at least three separate 2023–2025 papers indicates this is a recognized, named phenomenon in the field's own vocabulary — useful as a storyboard title-card phrase or voiceover motif since it is genuinely how the literature itself describes the problem, not an invented dramatization.

### Gaps
- Could not fetch full text of the ESMO Open "data tsunami" article (403 Forbidden) to pull a precise attributable quote/statistic from the body — only the framing/title is confirmed.

## Are there statistics on the shortage of clinical genomics/bioinformatics expertise available to support oncologists at the point of care?

### Takeaway
The shortage of combined clinical-genomics-and-bioinformatics expertise is repeatedly described in the literature as a "critical limiting factor" and workforce gap, but this research pass found strong qualitative statements rather than a hard quantified shortage statistic (e.g., a specific ratio of bioinformaticians to oncologists or number of unfilled positions) — that numeric gap should be flagged for the storyboard team.

### Cited Findings
- "A major obstacle is the shortage of experts with specialized knowledge in multiomics analysis, bioinformatics, and clinical interpretation. The shortage of professionals with combined genomics and clinical interpretation skills is a critical limiting factor" — surfaced via aggregated search of the point-of-care bioinformatics-shortage literature (candidate primary sources include the AMP Clinical Bioinformatician Body of Knowledge paper and/or the Frontiers "Advancing precision oncology with AI-powered genomic analysis" 2025 review; the exact originating paper for this specific sentence was not individually pinned down in this pass).
- "The number of data points per patient considered at the point-of-care in precision cancer medicine continues to increase... accompanied by a growing challenge of translating these observations into clinical insights. This is a time-intensive and laborious process for oncology professionals and molecular tumour boards" — same aggregated-search caveat as above; needs primary-source pinning before citing as a standalone quote.
- The Association for Molecular Pathology (AMP) published a "Body of Knowledge" steering-committee paper in 2024 explicitly because "there is currently no established body of knowledge that a bioinformatician must have developed before or acquired through training after joining a clinical molecular diagnostics laboratory" — i.e., the field lacks even a standardized competency framework for the bioinformaticians oncology depends on, which is itself evidence of an immature/understaffed support pipeline — [Development of a Body of Knowledge for the Clinical Bioinformatician, PMC, 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13169022/) (confirmed via direct fetch; no specific numeric workforce-gap statistic was present in the accessible portion of this article).
- A 2025 PMC paper on building a "genomics-capable cancer workforce" argues that as genomic technologies (multigene panels, liquid biopsy, transcriptomics, proteomics, integrated multi-omic biomarkers) become embedded in guidelines, "the demands placed on the cancer workforce have expanded substantially," with responsibilities now extending across oncology, pathology, laboratory medicine, nursing, pharmacy, genetics, bioinformatics, clinical research, ethics, policy, and health-system leadership — [Building a genomics-capable cancer workforce: a competency-based framework for education and training in precision oncology, PMC, 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13486856/)
- A trade-press piece flags a broader "shortage of medical geneticists" as a trend affecting cancer treatment, though the underlying quantified statistic was not independently verified in this pass — [Study Finds Shortage of Medical Geneticists, Cancer Therapy Advisor](https://www.cancertherapyadvisor.com/home/cancer-topics/general-oncology/medical-geneticists-shortage-trend-cancer-treatment/)

### Inferences
- The consistent, cross-paper theme is that the workforce/competency infrastructure (standardized training, defined bioinformatician role, adequate staffing) is lagging behind the pace at which multi-omic testing has been embedded into guidelines — this is a structural, not just individual-skill, gap, which strengthens the pitch's argument that computational tooling is filling an infrastructure void rather than just a training void.

### Gaps
- No single, hard, oft-cited statistic (e.g., "X bioinformaticians per Y oncology patients" or "X% of cancer centers lack access to a molecular geneticist/bioinformatician") was confirmed with a verified primary source in this research pass. This is the weakest-sourced of the five key questions and would benefit from a dedicated follow-up search (e.g., ASCO workforce surveys, AMP/CAP workforce reports, or NCI-designated cancer center staffing surveys) if a specific number is needed for the pitch deck.
