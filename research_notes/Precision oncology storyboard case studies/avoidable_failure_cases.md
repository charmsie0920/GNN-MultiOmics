# Avoidable Cancer Treatment Failures: Evidence for "Missed Genomic Match" Narrative Beat

**Scope note:** This research prioritizes sourcing rigor over volume, per assignment constraints. Findings are labeled by source type (peer-reviewed / advocacy / journalism) and causal language is hedged to match what each source actually claims. No source found makes an explicit, first-person causal claim of the form "this specific approved targeted drug, if given sooner, would have saved this specific patient" — that exact narrative beat does not appear to exist in a clean, ethically citable form. What does exist, documented below, is (a) one recent, named, journalistically verified case with a real delay-to-access gap and a poor outcome, reported with appropriate hedging by the outlet itself, and (b) strong aggregate/statistical evidence of the testing-gap phenomenon that make such cases statistically inevitable, plus several clinical case reports of alterations found only at progression/re-biopsy (not necessarily "missed" earlier — see hedges).

## Key Question 1: Published case reports / MTB retrospectives / advocacy accounts of a patient who failed standard chemo, where post-hoc sequencing found an actionable mutation with an approved targeted drug that wasn't given in time

### Takeaway
The strongest, most citable individual narrative found is a September 2026 KFF Health News investigative story (Mason Henderson) — a real patient, on the record, where genomic testing after standard treatment failure identified a plausible molecular target, but insurance denials delayed access to the drug for months and the patient died shortly after finally receiving it. This is journalism, not a peer-reviewed case report, and the drug was an **off-label, biologically-rationalized match** (not an FDA-approved indication for his cancer type), so causation must be hedged carefully. Separately, peer-reviewed case reports exist of actionable alterations (BRCA, ALK) detected only at disease progression/re-biopsy after chemotherapy — but these more often reflect **acquired/emergent resistance mutations** rather than "was there all along and should have been caught earlier," so they support the general "repeat/more complete testing changes management" beat rather than the stronger "missed at diagnosis" beat.

### Cited Findings
- **[Journalism — KFF Health News, republished by CBS News, Sept 21, 2026, byline Arthur Allen]**: Mason Henderson, 21, was diagnosed in March 2024 with diffuse hemispheric glioma (H3 G34-mutant), a rare brain cancer. Standard treatment (surgery removing 90% of tumor, 16 months of radiation/chemotherapy, and a clinical trial with craniospinal irradiation) failed to eliminate the cancer, which spread to his spinal cord by September 2025. Genetic/molecular analysis of his tumor led his neuro-oncologists (Jacob Mandel, Baylor College of Medicine; Jessica Schulte, NYU Langone) to identify olaparib (Lynparza, AstraZeneca/Merck) as a plausible targeted option, with Schulte stating cells in tumors like his "have a flaw that drugs like Lynparza can target." Insurance denied coverage twice (pharmacy benefit manager Liviniti on Jan 30, then the employer health plan); AstraZeneca's patient assistance program also initially rejected the request. After the mother's public social-media campaign, AstraZeneca approved compassionate access in March. Henderson received the drug for "nearly two months" before dying May 4, 2026. His mother: "Cancer don't pause while the paperwork's in progress." — [CBS News / KFF Health News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/rare-cancer-drugs-insurance/)
  - **Hedge:** The article explicitly frames this as an off-label, "biologically reasonable" molecular rationale for a PARP inhibitor in a rare cancer without an approved targeted therapy — not an approved on-label match that was simply denied. The outlet does not claim the drug would have saved him if given earlier; it documents the delay and the death as sequential facts, and quotes the mother's framing. Use this case for the "insurance/access-delay" beat, not for an "approved drug existed and was withheld" beat.
- **[Peer-reviewed case report — Cancers (Basel)/related case literature]**: A patient with ALK-wild-type lung adenocarcinoma who received first-line platinum-based chemotherapy plus VEGF/PD-L1 inhibitors developed an ALK fusion detected only on repeat testing at progression; switching to alectinib produced a partial response. The authors frame this as chemotherapy potentially enriching an ALK-fusion clone through selective pressure, and argue for the clinical importance of **repeat** genetic testing after progression — not necessarily a "missed at baseline" mutation. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12928097/)
- **[Peer-reviewed case report]**: A triple-negative breast cancer patient with no BRCA mutation detected initially progressed after 7.6 months on nab-paclitaxel; BRCA mutation was detected upon progression, and subsequent olaparib treatment reduced lung metastases. Framed by authors as an "emerging"/acquired mutation, not an initial testing miss. — [PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10988688/)
- **[Peer-reviewed retrospective cohort, Cancers (Basel), Dec 2021, Frank et al., DOI 10.3390/cancers14010132]**: In 92 advanced NSCLC patients re-biopsied at progression after first-line treatment (87 with successful genomic analysis), **85% (74/87) had at least one actionable molecular alteration (AMA)** revealed at progression. Roughly one-third of the AMAs were detected **only** at re-biopsy (not present/found at initial diagnosis). However, **only 5% of patients actually received targeted treatment** matched to these findings — the paper attributes this gap primarily to performance-status decline by the time of re-testing, not lack of drug availability. — [PMC8749927](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8749927/)
  - This is a strong quantitative anchor for "molecular matches exist but arrive too late to act on them" but it is NOT the same claim as "the mutation was present and testable at diagnosis and simply not ordered" — some AMAs are genuinely acquired resistance events.

### Inferences
- The clearest ethically-citable "avoidable loss" narrative available is the Mason Henderson KFF Health News story, but it is best framed around **access/insurance delay after a molecular rationale was identified**, not around "a targeted drug existed and testing simply wasn't ordered." A storyboard using it should not claim an approved, on-label drug was withheld — that would overstate the source.
- The peer-reviewed case-report literature more consistently supports "repeat biopsy/testing at progression reveals a match chemotherapy alone would miss" rather than "testing at diagnosis was skipped and a match was already there."

### Gaps
- I did not find a peer-reviewed case report or patient-advocacy narrative that unambiguously matches the exact requested pattern (failed standard chemo → later/retrospective sequencing → an **already-approved, on-label** targeted drug existed at time of initial diagnosis → but standard-of-care testing gaps meant it was never given). This may exist in the literature (e.g., in JCO Precision Oncology case series or COLONTOWN/FORCE community storytelling) but was not surfaced within the search budget for this task; flagging as a gap rather than stretching a weaker source, per instructions.
- Could not access LUNGevity's #KnowYourBiomarker/"No One Missed" individual patient stories in enough depth to determine whether any describe a *negative* outcome from a testing gap (the ones found — Tabitha Paccione/ALK, "Dan"/EGFR — appear to be **positive-outcome** survivor stories used to promote testing, not "avoidable loss" narratives). See Key Question 4 for detail.

## Key Question 2: Retrospective cohort studies quantifying how many patients had an actionable alteration never tested for, or found only after progression (NSCLC, CRC, breast)

### Takeaway
Large real-world cohort studies consistently show that roughly half or more of eligible advanced-cancer patients are not tested per guideline before first-line therapy, and that when testing does happen, it frequently happens late (after progression) rather than at diagnosis — a pattern documented across NSCLC, CRC, and multi-cancer real-world databases.

### Cited Findings
- **[Peer-reviewed, JAMA Network Open, April 2026, DOI 10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.5585]** — "Trends and Disparities in the Use of Next-Generation Sequencing in Patients With Cancer in the United States": Cohort of **63,294 patients** with advanced/metastatic breast, prostate, colorectal, pancreatic, and lung cancers (Flatiron Health EHR database, ~280 clinics/800 sites of care). **For most cancer types, roughly half of patients received genetic sequencing.** Median time to testing ranged from **<1 month in advanced NSCLC to 11.0 months in metastatic prostate cancer**. Lower socioeconomic status was associated with significantly longer time to testing in metastatic breast cancer, advanced NSCLC, and metastatic colorectal cancer; Black race, Hispanic ethnicity, and Medicaid/Medicare coverage were also associated with longer time to NGS. — [PMC13058762](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13058762/) / [JAMA Network Open](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.5585)
- **[Journalism, STAT News, April 7 2026]** — Summarizing the same/related study: over 40% of more than 63,000 US patients with advanced cancers received no genomic testing at all; some patients — including those who were Black, Hispanic, or low-income — waited longer for testing than others. — [STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2026/04/07/genomic-tests-needed-for-targeted-cancer-treatments/)
- **[Real-world data, NSCLC]**: Only **67% of all patients** and **77% of patients with nonsquamous, metastatic NSCLC** had ALK, EGFR, and PD-L1 testing within 90 days of diagnosis, per real-world claims/EHR data cited in industry/clinical-news coverage of biomarker testing patterns. — [AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/real-world-insights-on-biomarker-testing-patterns-and-implications-for-mnsclc-therapy-selection)
- **[Real-world data, NSCLC]**: Despite having insurance, **61.8% of mNSCLC patients did not receive biomarker testing** aligning with NCCN guidance for nonsquamous NSCLC within 90 days of initial diagnosis, per JCO Precision Oncology-related coverage. — [OncLive](https://www.onclive.com/view/gaps-and-future-directions-in-nsclc-biomarker-testing-liquid-biopsy-mrd-and-non-actionable-alterations) (I was unable to directly load the primary JCO Precision Oncology article, PMC11371103 — see Gaps)
- **[Real-world data, NSCLC, EGFR-specific]**: Nearly one-fourth of previously untreated stage IIIB–IV NSCLC patients in the US were not tested upfront for actionable EGFR mutations. — [OncologyNewsCentral](https://www.oncologynewscentral.com/nsclc/in-defiance-of-guidelines-many-patients-with-advanced-nsclc-not-tested-for-egfr)
- **[Peer-reviewed retrospective, molecular tumor board]**: In a real-world study of 124 molecular tumor board patients accrued **at progression** and lacking approved therapy options, actionable alterations were detected in **43.5%** of patients, though only **31%** had alterations that were both actionable and therapeutically unexploited (i.e., a real missed-opportunity subset). — cited via search summary of [PMC13142151](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13142151/) (not independently fetched/verified — see Gaps)
- **[CRC, real-world]**: A Flatiron Health database analysis of metastatic colorectal cancer patients found that guideline-recommended biomarker (RAS etc.) testing rates remain suboptimal, and are affected by age, ethnicity, race, socioeconomic status, practice setting, ECOG status, and year of diagnosis; RAS testing was often ordered only **after** first-line treatment had already started in a retrospective cohort. — [JNCI Cancer Spectrum](https://academic.oup.com/jncics/article/6/6/pkac065/6712321)
- **[Case series, sarcoma]**: A case-series-style report describes actionable genomic alterations guiding effective targeted therapy in rare sarcomas after prior lines failed, illustrating the general "late-stage sequencing changes management" pattern in a rare-tumor context. — [PMC13357230](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13357230/)

### Inferences
- Across NSCLC, CRC, and multi-cancer cohorts, the consistent real-world pattern is: (1) roughly 25–60% of guideline-eligible patients are not tested per NCCN timelines before first-line therapy, and (2) testing that does happen is frequently delayed relative to treatment initiation. This statistically guarantees that some meaningful fraction of "standard chemo failures" in these cohorts include patients who had an actionable, currently-druggable alteration that was never characterized before a non-targeted regimen was chosen.
- The MTB-progression cohort data (43.5% actionable, 31% "actionable and unexploited") is a good aggregate proxy statistic for the "missed target" narrative, but I could not independently verify the abstract's exact figures via direct fetch — treat as moderately-confident pending verification.

### Gaps
- I could not directly load the full text of the JCO Precision Oncology "Real-World Biomarker Test Ordering Practices in NSCLC" article (PMC11371103) — it returned a CAPTCHA wall on fetch — so the 61.8%/67%/77% figures above are sourced through secondary clinical-news coverage (AJMC, OncLive) rather than the primary article. These outlets are reputable oncology trade press summarizing peer-reviewed studies, but flagging the indirection for the report writer.
- Breast-cancer-specific real-world testing-gap statistics (analogous to the NSCLC/CRC figures above) were not directly surfaced in this pass; the JAMA Network Open multi-cancer study (above) includes breast cancer in its 63,294-patient cohort but breast-specific sub-rates were not extracted.

## Key Question 3: Statistics on time delay between diagnosis/progression and comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) results, and documented consequences of that delay

### Takeaway
CGP turnaround time has improved nationally (median ~43 days in 2018 to ~32 days in 2024) but still varies widely by site (23 days fastest quintile vs. 46.5 days slowest), and a peer-reviewed outcomes study directly links **timely** CGP (results available before first-line therapy starts) to substantially higher use of matched targeted therapy and to a significant delay in time-to-treatment-discontinuation among driver-positive patients — though it did not find a statistically significant overall survival difference.

### Cited Findings
- **[Peer-reviewed, JCO Oncology Practice, DOI 10.1200/OP-25-01191]**: Median CGP turnaround time across sites decreased from **43 days (2018) to 32 days (2024)**; fastest-quintile sites had median turnaround of **23.1 days** vs. **46.5 days** for the slowest quintile. The largest contributor to delay was the interval from biopsy to CGP being *ordered* (not lab processing time), ranging from a median of **10.0 days (fastest sites) to 30.8 days (slowest sites)**. — [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42314086/) / [Targeted Oncology summary](https://www.targetedonc.com/view/study-finds-genomic-profiling-turnaround-time-improves-but-delays-persist)
- **[Peer-reviewed, JCO Precision Oncology, 2024, Yorio et al., DOI 10.1200/PO.23.00292]**: In a cohort of **2,694 patients with advanced NSCLC**, timely CGP (available before first-line therapy started, vs. after) was associated with a **14-percentage-point increase in matched targeted therapy use (17% vs. 2.8%)** and a **14-percentage-point increase in precision immune-checkpoint-inhibitor use (18% vs. 3.9%)**. Among driver-positive patients (n=437), timely CGP was associated with significantly longer time to therapy discontinuation (median not reached/faster discontinuation in the delayed group: 10 months delayed vs. 3.9 months timely to discontinuation-relevant event framing per source; adjusted HR 0.50, p=2.0×10⁻⁶). Overall survival difference was **not statistically significant** (median 29 months delayed vs. 32 months timely; p=0.12). Timely CGP also produced an estimated **$13,659.37 per-patient reduction** in costs from ineffective ICI therapy. — [PMC10939592](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10939592/)
  - **Hedge:** this is the single strongest peer-reviewed source in this research pass linking testing timeliness directly to a treatment-selection outcome (targeted therapy uptake), but note the OS endpoint was NOT statistically significant — do not claim CGP timing was shown to extend survival; the significant findings are treatment-selection accuracy and time-to-discontinuation/cost, not OS.
- **[Journalism/advocacy framing, Inside Precision Medicine]**: Despite a 2018 Medicare coverage expansion for NGS, genomic testing adoption among Medicare beneficiaries with cancer rose only from **6.0% (2016) to 16.7% (2023)** — testing remains low even where covered. — [Inside Precision Medicine](https://www.insideprecisionmedicine.com/topics/oncology/genomic-testing-for-cancer-patients-still-rare-despite-medicare-coverage/)
- **[Secondary summary of a UPMC/Pitt access report]**: A characterization that "most genomics are being tested late in late lines of therapy, at a therapeutically futile stage" appears in coverage of insurance-barrier research on precision oncology access. — [Binay Tara Foundation / Cancer News summary](https://binaytara.org/cancernews/article/the-hidden-barrier-to-precision-oncology-how-insurance-policies-delay-access-to-genomic-testing) (secondary source; primary UPMC/Pitt report not independently verified in this pass)

### Inferences
- The Yorio et al. (2024) JCO Precision Oncology study is the best available peer-reviewed anchor for a storyboard beat about "testing delay → wrong drug chosen first → wasted time on ineffective therapy" — it directly quantifies both the treatment-selection consequence (targeted therapy use rate) and a time-based consequence (faster discontinuation in the delayed-testing group, implying more time spent on a drug not working), with an explicit dollar cost figure. It should NOT be used to claim a survival benefit, since that endpoint was non-significant.

### Gaps
- No source found quantifies, in a single clean statistic, "average number of weeks of disease progression that occurs during the wait for CGP results" — turnaround-time statistics and progression consequences are documented in separate literatures (turnaround-time studies vs. outcome-association studies), not merged into one delay-to-progression metric.

LUNGevity has an active, large-scale advocacy campaign ("No One Missed" / #KnowYourBiomarker) built explicitly around this theme, with disparity statistics on testing access, but the individual patient stories surfaced in this research are framed as **positive-outcome survivor testimonials promoting testing** rather than "avoidable loss" narratives. The strongest documented "access barrier → bad outcome" account remains the KFF Health News/CBS News story (Key Question 1), which is journalism, not patient-advocacy-org content.

### Cited Findings
- **[Advocacy, LUNGevity Foundation]**: LUNGevity's "Barriers to Biomarker Testing" initiative states: **"Patients on Medicaid are 40% less likely to get tested than those with private health insurance,"** and **"Medicaid patients are 30% less likely than patients who are privately insured to receive targeted therapies following testing."** The page frames this as a systemic-access problem (their "Project DIRECT" initiative), not via individual case narratives. — [LUNGevity](https://www.lungevity.org/research/patient-focused-research-center-patient-force/barriers-to-optimal-care/barriers-to)

This suggests journalism (KFF Health News, STAT News) rather than advocacy-org case libraries is the more productive source category for the specific "avoidable loss" narrative beat the student team wants.

## Key Question 5: Oncology literature/NCCN commentary on disparities/gaps in biomarker testing uptake (% of eligible NSCLC/CRC patients NOT tested before first-line therapy)

### Takeaway
Multiple independent real-world data sources converge on the same order-of-magnitude finding: roughly a quarter to two-thirds of guideline-eligible advanced NSCLC patients (depending on the specific study/definition/testing window) are not tested per NCCN-recommended timelines before first-line therapy, with CRC biomarker testing (RAS/RAS-family) also frequently occurring only after first-line treatment has already begun. Racial, socioeconomic, and insurance-type disparities are a consistent secondary finding across nearly every source.

### Cited Findings
- (Restating/consolidating from Key Question 2, all directly relevant here):
  - Only 67% of all NSCLC patients, 77% of nonsquamous metastatic NSCLC patients, had ALK/EGFR/PD-L1 testing within 90 days of diagnosis. — [AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/real-world-insights-on-biomarker-testing-patterns-and-implications-for-mnsclc-therapy-selection)
  - 61.8% of insured mNSCLC patients did not receive NCCN-guideline-concordant biomarker testing within 90 days of diagnosis. — [OncLive](https://www.onclive.com/view/gaps-and-future-directions-in-nsclc-biomarker-testing-liquid-biopsy-mrd-and-non-actionable-alterations)
  - Nearly one-fourth of untreated stage IIIB–IV NSCLC patients were not tested upfront for EGFR. — [OncologyNewsCentral](https://www.oncologynewscentral.com/nsclc/in-defiance-of-guidelines-many-patients-with-advanced-nsclc-not-tested-for-egfr)
  - CRC: RAS testing was "typically prescribed after first-line treatment initiation" in a retrospective cohort; non-testing attributed to test unavailability, financial, or medical reasons. — [PMC10081134](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10081134/); disparities confirmed by [JNCI Cancer Spectrum](https://academic.oup.com/jncics/article/6/6/pkac065/6712321)
  - Multi-cancer (breast/prostate/CRC/pancreatic/lung), 63,294 patients: roughly half tested overall, disparities by SES/race/insurance. — [JAMA Network Open 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.5585)
  - Medicare NGS adoption only 6.0%→16.7% (2016→2023) despite coverage expansion. — [Inside Precision Medicine](https://www.insideprecisionmedicine.com/topics/oncology/genomic-testing-for-cancer-patients-still-rare-despite-medicare-coverage/)
- **[Qualitative/access-barriers study]**: Providers and patients cite cost/insurance uncertainty as a top barrier; "many patients could not recall ever discussing testing with their doctor"; guideline evolution has outrun individual physicians' capacity to keep testing orders complete and current. — summarized from search results referencing a qualitative study of Black cancer patients and oncology providers, [Current Oncology / doi.org/10.3390/curroncol33070413](https://doi.org/10.3390/curroncol33070413) (not independently fetched — see Gaps)
- **[Policy]**: State-level biomarker-testing-coverage legislation exists in Rhode Island, Washington State, and Mississippi, mandating insurance coverage of evidence-based molecular testing — implying that in most states, no such floor exists. (Source: same search synthesis as above; primary legislative text not verified in this pass.)

### Inferences
- The magnitude and consistency of the testing-gap statistic across independently-run studies (different databases: Flatiron/JAMA Network Open, JCO Precision Oncology real-world claims data, JNCI Cancer Spectrum for CRC) make this one of the most solidly-evidenced parts of this research assignment — safe to present as a well-established, peer-reviewed real-world finding, not a single-study artifact.
- I did not find an NCCN-authored commentary/editorial specifically quantifying testing-gap percentages (NCCN Guidelines themselves set the testing *standard* that these real-world studies measure adherence against, but NCCN as an organization does not appear to publish its own gap-rate statistics — those come from independent real-world-evidence researchers using claims/EHR data).

### Gaps
- Could not verify the Current Oncology qualitative-study citation and the state biomarker-legislation claim via direct fetch (both came through WebSearch synthesis only); flagging for the report writer to treat as lower-confidence pending independent verification if used prominently.
- Breast-cancer-specific (HER2/BRCA) testing-gap percentages analogous to the NSCLC/CRC figures above were not directly extracted in this pass, despite breast cancer being included in the JAMA Network Open 63,294-patient cohort.
