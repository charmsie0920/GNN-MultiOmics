# Matched (Genomically-Guided) vs. Unmatched/Standard-of-Care Cancer Therapy: Citable Statistics

## What were the ORR/PFS results comparing matched vs non-matched therapy arms in the MD Anderson IMPACT and COMPASS trials (Tsimberidou, Kurzrock, Andre, et al.)?

### Takeaway
The MD Anderson IMPACT program (a large non-randomized precision-oncology registry, not a randomized trial) consistently shows roughly 3x higher objective response rates and significantly longer PFS/OS for patients who received genomically matched targeted therapy vs. those with an actionable alteration who received non-matched therapy — but IMPACT is confounded (not randomized) and its own follow-up randomized trial (IMPACT2) did not confirm a survival benefit. "COMPASS" as described in the assignment (Andre/Kurzrock) could not be verified as a real matched-vs-unmatched breast/general trial; the only "COMPASS" trial found in the literature is a distinct, differently-designed pancreatic cancer genomics trial (Aung/Knox et al., Toronto) that does not compare matched vs. unmatched arms in the way the question implies.

### Cited Findings
- **IMPACT (Tsimberidou et al., long-term follow-up, *Journal of Hematology & Oncology*, 2019):** Of 3,487 patients screened, 1,307 (37.5%) had ≥1 actionable molecular alteration; 711 (54.4%) received matched targeted therapy and 596 (45.6%) received non-matched therapy. Objective response rate: 16.4% (matched) vs. 5.4% (non-matched). Disease control rate (response + stable disease ≥6 months): 35.3% (matched) vs. 20.3% (non-matched). Median PFS: 4.0 months (matched) vs. 2.8 months (non-matched), p<0.0001. Median OS: 9.3 months (matched) vs. 7.3 months (non-matched). 3-year OS: 15% (matched) vs. 7% (non-matched). 10-year OS: 6% (matched) vs. 1% (non-matched). Hazard ratio for OS = 0.72 (p<0.001), with matched therapy an independent predictor of longer OS on multivariate analysis. — [Long-term overall survival and prognostic score predicting survival: the IMPACT study in precision medicine, J Hematol Oncol 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6937824/) (fetched and read directly)
- A separate secondary/press summary of IMPACT (population framed slightly differently: 3,743 patients tested, 1,307 (34.9%) with ≥1 targetable alteration) reports the same 711/596 matched/non-matched split and the same 3-year (15% vs 7%) / 10-year (6% vs 1%) OS figures, median OS 9.3 vs 7.3 months, HR 0.72 — [IMPACT Study Shows Benefit of Matched Targeted Therapies, Targeted Oncology](https://www.targetedonc.com/view/impact-study-shows-benefit-of-matched-targeted-therapies-for-hardtotreat-cancers); [IMPACT: Matching Treatment and Molecular Testing Extends Survival, AHDB](https://www.ahdbonline.com/articles/impact-matching-treatment-and-molecular-testing-extends-survival-in-advanced-cancer). Note the minor discrepancy in total screened (3,487 vs 3,743) and actionable-alteration percentage (37.5% vs 34.9%) between sources reporting on what appears to be the same underlying cohort — likely different analysis cutoffs/publications from the same IMPACT program; treat the directly-fetched J Hematol Oncol 2019 numbers as the primary citation.
- One AI-generated search summary (not independently verified against primary text) cited a different IMPACT response-rate pair of 16.2% (matched) vs. 5.2% (non-matched), very close to but not identical to the 16.4%/5.4% figures above — likely rounding/paraphrase of the same statistic, or possibly drawn from an earlier IMPACT publication (e.g., Tsimberidou et al. 2012, Clin Cancer Res, the original smaller cohort). This could not be independently confirmed in this session — [search-derived, unconfirmed primary source].
- **IMPACT2 (randomized follow-up study):** A randomized trial (IMPACT2) was conducted to more rigorously test matched vs. non-matched therapy; per commentary, IMPACT2 raised "challenges and opportunities" for precision oncology — implying it did not straightforwardly replicate IMPACT's registry-based benefit. Exact ORR/PFS/OS numbers for IMPACT2 were not retrieved in this session. — [Challenges and opportunities associated with the MD Anderson IMPACT2 randomized study, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9612600/); [Precision medicine: preliminary results from IMPACT2, npj Precision Oncology](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41698-021-00159-2)
- **"COMPASS" trial (Aung/Knox/O'Kane et al., pancreatic cancer, not confirmed as an Andre/Kurzrock trial):** 268 patients with advanced pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) received mFOLFIRINOX or gemcitabine-nab-paclitaxel per physician choice; 63 patients underwent tumor biopsy with whole-genome/RNA sequencing successful in 98%/95%; ~30% had potentially actionable genetic alterations. Median OS: 10.6 months (mFOLFIRINOX) vs. 8.4 months (gemcitabine-nab-paclitaxel) — this is a chemotherapy regimen comparison, not a matched-vs-unmatched targeted-therapy comparison. — [Genomics-Driven Precision Medicine for Advanced Pancreatic Cancer: Early Results from the COMPASS Trial, Clin Cancer Res 2018 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29288237/)
- A related but distinct pilot study (co-authored by Razelle Kurzrock) found genomically matched targeted-agent combinations were active in advanced pancreatic cancer, but exact ORR/PFS/OS figures for a matched-vs-unmatched comparison were not retrieved — [Personalized matched targeted therapy in advanced pancreatic cancer: a pilot cohort analysis, PMC](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9860045/)

### Inferences
- The ~3x higher ORR (16.4% vs 5.4%) and roughly 40% relative PFS improvement (4.0 vs 2.8 months) for matched vs. non-matched therapy in IMPACT is a real, citable, directly-verified statistic, but it comes from a large non-randomized registry where treatment assignment was not random (patients who got "matched" therapy may differ systematically — e.g., better performance status, access to trials — from those who got "non-matched" therapy), so it should be described as "associated with" rather than proven causal in a pitch, unless paired with the OS hazard ratio (0.72) from multivariate-adjusted analysis, which is the strongest causal-inference claim available from this dataset.
- The assignment's framing of "COMPASS" as an Andre/Kurzrock matched-vs-unmatched trial does not match what is findable in the literature; the team should either drop this citation or clarify which specific COMPASS trial (there may be multiple trials using this acronym, e.g., a breast cancer SAFIR/COMPASS-related trial not surfaced here) they mean before using it in the pitch.

### Gaps
- Could not confirm a trial literally named "COMPASS" led by Andre and/or Kurzrock with a matched-vs-unmatched design as implied by the prompt; only the Toronto pancreatic-cancer COMPASS trial (different design, different authors) was found. This should be flagged to the team as likely a misremembered trial name/pairing.
- IMPACT2 (the randomized confirmatory trial) exact ORR/PFS/OS comparison numbers were not retrieved — only commentary describing it as raising "challenges and opportunities," which suggests results were less clean than the original IMPACT registry. This is an important nuance for the pitch (don't overstate registry-based findings as randomized-trial-confirmed) and should be dug into further if time allows.
- The discrepancy between the 3,487-screened/37.5%-actionable figure (directly fetched) and the 3,743-screened/34.9%-actionable figure (from secondary press coverage) was not resolved to a single authoritative number.

## What were the match rate and outcome statistics from the NCI-MATCH trial?

### Takeaway
NCI-MATCH screened nearly 6,000 refractory-cancer patients with next-generation sequencing; roughly 1 in 3 had an "actionable" alteration, but only about 1 in 6 to 1 in 4 were actually assigned to a matched treatment arm (due to slot availability, eligibility, and clinical deterioration before assignment) — and even among those treated, the pooled objective response rate across substudies was modest (~26%), with wide variation by arm (from single digits up to 33–36% in the best-responding arms).

### Cited Findings
- NCI-MATCH (EAY131) screened 5,954 patients with refractory malignant solid tumors, lymphomas, or myeloma at 1,117 accrual sites, using centralized NGS and selected immunohistochemistry. Molecular profiling was successful in 93.0% of specimens. An actionable alteration was identified in 37.6% of patients (JCO 2020 reports this specific figure; other coverage rounds to "nearly two of five patients," i.e., ~38%). After clinical and molecular exclusion criteria were applied, 17.8% of patients were actually assigned to a treatment arm; up to 26.4% theoretically could have been assigned had all 38 subprotocols been simultaneously open/available. — [Molecular Landscape and Actionable Alterations in a Genomically Guided Cancer Clinical Trial: NCI-MATCH, JCO 2020 (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33048619/); [NCI-MATCH Trial Identifies Actionable Alterations in More Than One-Third of Patients, Cancer Therapy Advisor](https://www.cancertherapyadvisor.com/features/molecular-alteration-cancer-treatment-risk-nci-match/)
- 1,593 patients were ultimately assigned across 38 substudies (arms) of the trial, out of the total screened population, over the trial's full run from 2015 to its completion in 2023. — [End of NCI-MATCH positions precision medicine for next big push, Frederick National Laboratory](https://frederick.cancer.gov/news/end-nci-match-trial-positions-precision-medicine-and-genetic-sequencing)
- Of the initial 27 published substudies, 7 (25.9%) met their pre-specified positive signal-seeking objective (i.e., "positive" trials), yielding an average reported response rate around 25.9% across published studies to date. — [multiple search-aggregated sources; see also Nature Medicine, "The NCI-MATCH trial: lessons for precision oncology"](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-023-02379-4)
- Individual high-performing arms: Arm H (dabrafenib + trametinib in BRAF V600E-mutant non-melanoma tumors) met its primary endpoint with an objective response rate of 33%. — [Dabrafenib and Trametinib in Patients With Tumors With BRAFV600E Mutations: NCI-MATCH Subprotocol H (PubMed)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32758030/)
- Nivolumab in tumors with DNA mismatch-repair deficiency (excluding colon cancer) had a confirmed objective response rate of 36% (15 of 42 patients). — [search-aggregated; NCI-MATCH press materials, ECOG-ACRIN](https://ecog-acrin.org/clinical-trials/eay131-nci-match-precision-medicine/nci-match-eay131-press-center/)

### Inferences
- The gap between "actionable alteration found" (37.6%) and "actually assigned to matched treatment" (17.8%) is itself a citable, important statistic for a pitch about real-world limits of precision oncology: roughly half of patients with a theoretically targetable mutation in NCI-MATCH never actually received a matched drug, mainly due to trial-slot/logistics and clinical decline — a distinct issue from "does matched therapy work," and a strong data point for a pitch emphasizing the gap between genomic potential and clinical delivery.
- The pooled ~26–34% response rate range across positive NCI-MATCH substudies is in the same general range as the IMPACT registry's higher-end matched-response figures, but NCI-MATCH is single-arm (no randomized non-matched comparator within each substudy), so it cannot by itself produce a "matched vs. unmatched" comparative statistic — it should be cited as an absolute response-rate benchmark, not a head-to-head comparison.

### Gaps
- Could not retrieve a direct NCI-MATCH statistic contrasting patients who received matched therapy vs. a formal unmatched/standard-of-care control arm within the same trial, because NCI-MATCH's design is single-arm per substudy (patients are their own historical comparison via refractory status), not randomized matched-vs-unmatched.
- Could not verify overall survival or PFS pooled figures across all NCI-MATCH substudies (only response-rate figures were found for aggregate/arm-level results).

## What match/response statistics came out of TAPUR (ASCO's Targeted Agent and Profiling Utilization Registry)?

### Takeaway
TAPUR is an ongoing non-randomized registry (not primarily a comparative matched-vs-unmatched trial) testing off-label targeted drugs matched to genomic alterations found on routine commercial testing; roughly three-quarters of registrants who undergo genomic testing are successfully matched to and enrolled on a study cohort, and response/disease-control rates vary enormously by tumor type and target — from near-zero to notably positive signals in specific biomarker-defined subgroups.

### Cited Findings
- Among 3,448 individuals registered in a TAPUR analysis of targetable genomic alteration prevalence, 25% were ultimately not enrolled on the study for reasons including ineligibility, no matching treatment option, or cohort unavailability — implying roughly 75% were successfully enrolled/matched to a cohort. — [Prevalence of targetable genomic alterations among a diverse population participating in the ASCO TAPUR Study, npj Precision Oncology 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12229528/)
- As of the most recent public tally (September 2023), TAPUR has published results on 34 treatment groups (cohorts); 21 of 34 (61.8%) reported "positive" findings (met the pre-specified signal-of-activity bar) and 13 (38.2%) reported "negative" findings. — [TAPUR Results Roll Out, Targeted Oncology](https://www.targetedonc.com/view/tapur-results-roll-out-bringing-potential-new-paradigms-into-focus)
- HER2-positive colorectal cancer cohort (pertuzumab + trastuzumab, n=28): objective response rate 25%, disease control rate 50%. — [Data From Colorectal Cancer Cohorts of TAPUR Study, ASCO Post](https://ascopost.com/news/january-2020/data-from-colorectal-cancer-cohorts-of-tapur-study-presented/)
- Microsatellite-stable colorectal cancer cohort (pembrolizumab): disease control rate 50% (90% CI 36–60%), objective response rate 14%. — [ASCO Post coverage](https://ascopost.com/issues/march-10-2020/tapur-study-biomarker-driven-treatment-paying-off-in-colorectal-cancer/)
- Soft tissue sarcoma cohort (palbociclib): disease control rate 48%, objective response rate 3.7% — illustrating that "matched" therapy does not guarantee meaningful response even when a biomarker is present. — [Targeted Oncology coverage](https://www.targetedonc.com/view/tapur-results-roll-out-bringing-potential-new-paradigms-into-focus)
- High tumor-mutational-burden metastatic breast cancer cohort (pembrolizumab): reported in JCO — exact response-rate figure not retrieved in this session, only that responses were observed ("Pembrolizumab Produces Responses in Pretreated Advanced Cancers With High Tumor Mutation Burden"). — [Pembrolizumab in Patients With Metastatic Breast Cancer With High Tumor Mutational Burden: TAPUR Study, JCO](https://ascopubs.org/doi/full/10.1200/JCO.20.02923)

### Inferences
- TAPUR's "positive in 21/34 (61.8%) of published cohorts" statistic is a reasonable, directly citable figure for "how often does molecularly matched off-label therapy show a meaningful signal" — but it describes cohort-level trial success (i.e., did enough individual patients respond to declare the cohort's drug-biomarker pairing worth pursuing further), not patient-level response rate, and should not be conflated with an individual patient's probability of benefit.
- The wide spread of TAPUR cohort response rates (3.7% in sarcoma/palbociclib up to 25%+ in HER2+ CRC) is itself a useful statistic to cite for the point that "molecularly matched" is not synonymous with "highly effective" — it depends heavily on the specific biomarker-drug pairing.

### Gaps
- Could not find a TAPUR-wide pooled ORR/PFS/OS statistic across all cohorts (TAPUR reports results cohort-by-cohort, not as a single aggregate matched-vs-unmatched trial-wide statistic), so no single "TAPUR overall response rate" figure exists to cite the way IMPACT's pooled figures can be cited.
- No direct TAPUR comparison to a contemporaneous standard-of-care/unmatched control arm was found (TAPUR is single-arm per cohort by design).

## Are there systematic reviews or meta-analyses quantifying the benefit of precision oncology / molecularly matched therapy vs. standard chemotherapy across trials?

### Takeaway
Yes — the most authoritative and most-cited meta-analysis is Schwaederle et al. (JCO 2015), which pooled 570 phase II single-agent trials (32,149 patients, 2010–2012) and found personalized/biomarker-matched strategies roughly tripled response rate and more than doubled PFS compared to non-personalized strategies; a more recent (2024) systematic review of real-world molecular tumor board (MTB) data across 12,176 patients found similar directional benefit with hazard ratios below 1 for both OS and PFS favoring molecularly tailored therapy.

### Cited Findings
- **Schwaederle et al., "Impact of Precision Medicine in Diverse Cancers: A Meta-Analysis of Phase II Clinical Trials," *Journal of Clinical Oncology*, 2015 (also republished/related as "Association of Biomarker-Based Treatment Strategies With Response Rates and Progression-Free Survival in Refractory Malignant Neoplasms: A Meta-analysis"):** Reviewed 570 phase II single-agent studies, 32,149 patients, published Jan 1, 2010–Dec 31, 2012. Personalized (biomarker-matched) approach vs. non-personalized approach: median response rate 31% vs. 10.5% (p<0.001); median PFS 5.9 vs. 2.7 months. One search-aggregated summary additionally reported median OS 13.7 vs. 8.9 months for the same comparison, though this OS figure was not independently confirmed against the primary abstract in this session and should be verified before citing with the same confidence as the RR/PFS figures. — [Impact of Precision Medicine in Diverse Cancers: A Meta-Analysis of Phase II Clinical Trials, JCO 2015 (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4737863/); [related PubMed abstract, Association of Biomarker-Based Treatment Strategies with Response Rates and PFS](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27273579/?dopt=Abstract)
- **Systematic review and meta-analysis of molecular tumor board (MTB) data, *npj Precision Oncology*, 2024/2025:** Included 34 studies (of 576 screened), published 2020–January 2024, covering 12,176 patients across 26 major cancer types. 20.8% (2,532 patients) received molecularly tailored ("matched") therapy following MTB review. Among 178 reported outcome measures: median OS 13.5 months, median PFS 4.5 months, ORR range 5–57% (reflecting large heterogeneity by cancer type/target). Comparative (matched vs. non-matched) hazard ratios: OS HR 0.46 (95% CI 0.28–0.76, p<0.001) and PFS HR 0.65 (95% CI 0.52–0.80, p<0.001), both favoring molecularly tailored therapy. — [Systematic review and meta-analysis of molecular tumor board data on clinical effectiveness and evaluation gaps, npj Precision Oncology (PMC)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11965449/)
- **Haslem et al., "A Retrospective Analysis of Precision Medicine Outcomes in Patients With Advanced Cancer Reveals Improved Progression-Free Survival Without Increased Health Care Costs" and its follow-up "Precision oncology in advanced cancer patients improves overall survival with lower weekly healthcare costs," *Oncotarget*, 2018:** Matched retrospective cohort study, single institution; 22 patients receiving genomically matched targeted therapy vs. 22 historically matched controls receiving standard chemotherapy (n=17) or best supportive care (n=5). Median OS: 51.7 weeks (matched/targeted) vs. 25.8 weeks (control), p=0.008. Average healthcare costs: $2,720/week (matched) vs. $3,453/week (control), p=0.036. — [Precision oncology in advanced cancer patients improves overall survival with lower weekly healthcare costs, Oncotarget 2018 (PMC)](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5844748/) — **Note: this is a very small (n=44 total), single-institution, retrospective, historically-controlled study; it should be cited as supportive/illustrative, not as a large or randomized evidence base.**

### Inferences
- The Schwaederle 2015 JCO meta-analysis (31% vs. 10.5% response rate; 5.9 vs. 2.7 months PFS) is the single strongest, most defensible "systematic review" statistic available for the pitch — it is large (32,149 patients, 570 trials), peer-reviewed, and specifically designed to compare personalized vs. non-personalized strategies. This should likely be the team's headline replacement statistic for the "70%" claim, reframed accurately as: "a meta-analysis of 570 phase II oncology trials found biomarker-matched therapy nearly tripled response rates (31% vs. 10.5%) and more than doubled PFS (5.9 vs. 2.7 months) compared to non-personalized therapy (Schwaederle et al., JCO 2015)."
- The 2024 MTB meta-analysis's OS/PFS hazard ratios (0.46 and 0.65) are a good complementary "real-world, more recent" statistic, but note its matched population is real-world/heterogeneous (26 cancer types, MTB-selected patients), so absolute numbers (13.5 months OS) shouldn't be presented as generalizable to all cancer patients — it's a population that was already referred to a molecular tumor board, typically after standard options had been exhausted.

### Gaps
- Could not independently verify the 13.7 vs. 8.9 months OS figure attributed to Schwaederle 2015 (this came from an AI search summary, not a directly fetched primary source — the JCO page returned a 403 error and could not be fetched directly). The report writer should treat this specific OS figure as unconfirmed pending direct access to the paper.
- Did not find a broader review answering "how many precision oncology meta-analyses exist" beyond these two — there is likely additional Nature Reviews Clinical Oncology or JAMA Oncology systematic-review literature not surfaced in the time available (e.g., a Cochrane-style review specific to genomically matched therapy).

## What statistics exist on how often standard NCCN/ESMO guideline-recommended first-line therapy fails, is discontinued for lack of efficacy, or produces no meaningful response — particularly in solid tumors / late-stage or refractory cancer?

### Takeaway
Concrete, well-sourced statistics on standard/guideline first-line therapy performance in advanced solid tumors exist and are considerably worse than commonly assumed for later lines of therapy, but no reliable source was found supporting a specific "70% of guideline-recommended treatments fail" statistic; the available data instead show guideline non-adherence itself is common (~30% of advanced NSCLC patients did not receive NCCN-adherent first-line therapy in one large study) and that response/outcome rates decline sharply in second/third-line refractory settings.

### Cited Findings
- In a study of 15,898 patients with advanced NSCLC, only 69.9% received NCCN-guideline-adherent first-line therapy (i.e., ~30.1% did not). Guideline-adherent patients had significantly lower risk of treatment discontinuation than non-adherent patients (HR 0.60, 95% CI 0.57–0.62) and longer median time to treatment discontinuation (3.45 vs. 2.40 months). — [First-line systemic therapy practice patterns and concordance with NCCN guidelines for patients diagnosed with metastatic NSCLC treated at NCCN institutions (PubMed/JNCCN)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22773800/); [JNCCN PDF](https://jnccn.org/downloadpdf/journals/jnccn/10/7/article-p847.pdf)
- For advanced NSCLC patients without an actionable driver mutation (i.e., ineligible for targeted therapy, treated with standard platinum-based chemotherapy per guidelines), roughly 70% can achieve clinical remission or disease stabilization initially, but nearly all eventually progress and require subsequent lines of therapy. In the second- or third-line refractory setting, outcomes are markedly worse: overall survival 7–9 months, PFS 2–4 months, objective response rate <10%. — [search-aggregated summary of NSCLC second/third-line refractory outcomes literature; specific primary citation not independently confirmed in this session]
- Ongoing, well-documented failure to deliver guideline-concordant care specifically in pancreatic cancer has been described as a persistent, quantified problem, though the exact percentage of patients affected was not extracted in this session. — [Ongoing Failure to Deliver Guideline-Concordant Care for Patients with Pancreatic Cancer, PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11763659/)

### Inferences
- The "69.9% NCCN-adherent" / "~30% non-adherent" NSCLC statistic is about guideline **adherence by physicians/systems**, not about guideline-recommended therapy **failing when given as directed** — these are conceptually different claims that are easy to conflate. The student team's "70% of cases" claim, if it originated from a garbled memory of a figure like this, would be a significant misreading: "~70% of patients got the guideline-recommended regimen" is nearly the opposite of "~70% of guideline-recommended regimens fail."
- The low objective response rate (<10%) in second-/third-line refractory NSCLC is a legitimate, strong, and separately-citable statistic for "how badly standard care performs in late-stage, heavily pretreated disease" — but this population (refractory, post multiple lines) is a small, specific subset of all cancer patients, not "standard cancer guidelines" broadly, and must be labeled as such if used in the pitch.

### Gaps
- Could not find a rigorous, peer-reviewed, tumor-agnostic statistic directly answering "what percentage of patients on NCCN/ESMO-guideline first-line therapy fail to respond or progress," as this figure varies enormously by cancer type, stage, and specific regimen and does not appear to be reported as a single aggregate number in the literature (each tumor type/line has its own reported response/PFS/OS statistics — there is no single "guideline failure rate" summary statistic).
- Did not locate a specific, citable "discontinuation for lack of efficacy" rate broken out separately from discontinuation for toxicity or progression in a large, guideline-adherent solid-tumor cohort; this would require deeper drug-specific or registry-specific research (e.g., SEER-Medicare or ASCO CancerLinQ discontinuation-reason data) not completed in this session.

## Is there a traceable origin for figures like "70% of patients don't respond to first prescribed drug"? Is it legitimate, misattributed, or unsupported for oncology specifically?

### Takeaway
The "70%" (and related 30–90%) figures trace back to general pharmacogenomics literature — specifically Spear, Heath-Chiozzi & Huff, *Trends in Molecular Medicine*, 2001, and its amplification by Allen Roses (then GlaxoSmithKline VP of Genetics) in a widely reported December 2003 statement to the London *Independent*/*Times* — and describe **average drug response variability across many major drug classes in medicine generally** (not cancer specifically, and not "guideline failure"). Within that same 2001 Spear paper, oncology drugs are reported as having the *lowest* average response rate among drug classes cited (25%), which — if anything — is a stronger and more specific statistic for the student team than a generic "70%" figure, but it is also very old (2001) and does not describe genomically-matched vs. unmatched comparisons. The commonly circulated "70% of patients don't respond to the first prescribed drug" framing appears to be a popularized, imprecise derivative of this pharmacogenomics literature, not a specific, rigorously sourced oncology or precision-oncology statistic — **it should be flagged to the team as weakly sourced/likely misattributed if used to describe cancer guideline failure specifically.**

### Cited Findings
- Spear BB, Heath-Chiozzi M, Huff J. "Clinical application of pharmacogenetics." *Trends in Molecular Medicine*, 2001;7(5):201-204. This paper presents a chart of average drug efficacy by therapeutic/drug class showing high variability — reported in secondary sources as ranging from about 25% to 80% effective depending on drug class, with cancer drugs cited as having the lowest average response rate (~25%) among the classes shown, and other summaries describing the range as "38% to 75% of patients fail to respond to a given treatment" across drug classes. — [PubMed record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11325631/); [full paper PDF](http://pkmp.org.pl/assets/31/62/58/66acc37140612d12d81c8ea5b44d3bd33208e4ad.pdf)
- Allen Roses, then Worldwide Vice President of Genetics at GlaxoSmithKline, was quoted in the London *Independent* (December 2003) stating that "the vast majority of drugs — more than 90 percent — only work in 30 or 50 percent of the people," and that fewer than half of patients prescribed some of the most expensive drugs actually derived benefit. This statement, not a peer-reviewed statistic itself, is widely credited with popularizing the "most drugs don't work in most people" framing that later gets rounded/mutated into round numbers like "70%" in secondary and tertiary sources. — [Glaxo exec admits drugs ineffective, UPI, Dec 2003](https://www.upi.com/Science_News/2003/12/08/Glaxo-exec-admits-drugs-ineffective/73021070894634/); [Glaxo Chief: "Most Prescription Medications Don't Work," secondary compilation](https://www.rejuvenation-science.com/research-news/prescription-drug-side-effects-statins-see-below-/n-drugs-performance)
- Separately, and specific to precision **oncology** (not general pharmacogenomics): Marquart J, Chan EY, Prasad V. "Estimation of the Percentage of US Patients With Cancer Who Benefit From Genome-Driven Oncology." *JAMA Oncology*, 2018;4(8):1093-1098. This paper estimated the percentage of US cancer patients eligible for and likely to benefit from an FDA-approved genome-targeted drug — reported in secondary coverage as roughly 5% of all new cancer patients as of the analysis, trending upward at roughly 0.5 percentage points/year from 2006 to 2018. This is the inverse framing of the same underlying idea (how few, not how many, patients benefit from precision oncology) and was central to a public debate with precision-oncology proponents (a formal rebuttal, "Moving Precision Oncology Forward Amid Myths and Misconceptions," was published in response). — [PubMed abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29710180/); [rebuttal, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30419079/); [reply, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30419080/)
- A related, frequently cited 2018 op-ed/commentary framing ("Precision medicine fails for up to 93% of patients. Are its proponents selling 'false hope'?") summarized a Kaiser Health News/NYT piece by Liz Szabo arguing that success-story coverage of precision oncology omits how often it does not work — this is journalism/commentary built on top of statistics like Prasad's, not a new primary data source, and the "93%" figure is the complement of an estimate like Prasad's ~5-9% benefit figure, not an independently derived statistic. — [Advisory Board Daily Briefing, Sept 21, 2018](https://www.advisory.com/daily-briefing/2018/09/21/precision-medicine)
- Vinay Prasad has separately been quoted estimating that genomically-targeted cancer drugs benefit approximately 5-9% of patients, depending on how the eligible population is defined — a view that was directly disputed by precision-oncology researchers (e.g., a 2017 Nature commentary titled "Debunking the Delusion That Precision Oncology Is an Illusion") as unduly pessimistic and dependent on restrictive denominators (e.g., counting only FDA-labeled genome-driven drugs, excluding off-label/trial-based matched therapy of the kind IMPACT, NCI-MATCH, and TAPUR study). — [Debunking the Delusion That Precision Oncology Is an Illusion, PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28550030/); [Perspective: The precision-oncology illusion, Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/537S63a)

### Inferences
