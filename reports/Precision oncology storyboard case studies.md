# Replace the 70% myth with real evidence

The team's "70% of cases" claim has no traceable source in oncology literature — it appears to be a garbled descendant of a 2001 pharmacogenomics chart and a 2003 GlaxoSmithKline executive's remark about drug response *across all of medicine*, not cancer guidelines specifically ([Spear et al., Trends in Molecular Medicine 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11325631/); [UPI, Dec 2003](https://www.upi.com/Science_News/2003/12/08/Glaxo-exec-admits-drugs-ineffective/73021070894634/)) — and should be retired, not re-sourced. Fortunately, the real data is more dramatic and fully citable: a 2015 meta-analysis of 570 phase II trials covering **32,149 patients** found biomarker-matched therapy nearly **tripled response rates (31% vs. 10.5%)** and more than doubled progression-free survival versus non-personalized therapy ([Schwaederle et al., JCO 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4737863/)). Two named, multi-source-verified patient stories — jazz musician Michael Wolff, saved by an off-label MEK inhibitor after genomic sequencing revealed his "lymphoma" was actually a rare MAP2K1-driven blood cancer ([NEJM 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMc1511490); [CBS News 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/cancer-genetic-sequencing-research-histiocytic-sarcoma-michael-wolff/)), and leukemia researcher Dr. Lukas Wartman, whose own RNA sequencing found an overexpressed gene targetable by a kidney-cancer drug never before tried on leukemia ([New York Times, 2012](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/07/08/health/in-gene-sequencing-treatment-for-leukemia-glimpses-of-the-future.html)) — supply exactly the emotional arc a "Dr. Alina / Sarah" storyboard needs. On the burden side, peer-reviewed surveys show only **14.6% of oncologists** correctly interpret variant-of-uncertain-significance case vignettes ([Macklin et al., Familial Cancer 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29721668/)) and **62.9% of Australian oncologists** say they need interpretive assistance with genomic reports ([Oncotarget 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8522847/)) — real, citable grounding for Dr. Alina's overwhelm. And on the "avoidable loss" front, over **40% of 63,294 advanced-cancer patients** in a 2026 JAMA Network Open study received no genomic testing at all, with the gap falling hardest on lower-income and minority patients ([JAMA Network Open 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.5585); [STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2026/04/07/genomic-tests-needed-for-targeted-cancer-treatments/)) — the statistical backbone for Sarah's near-miss. Every figure below is labeled by source type (peer-reviewed, journalism, or advocacy) so the team can calibrate confidence before it goes on a slide.

## Two verified patient sagas anchor the emotional arc

The single strongest individual case available to the team is **Michael Wolff**, a professional jazz musician whose tumor was initially diagnosed as lymphoma. Comprehensive genomic sequencing through Memorial Sloan Kettering's MSK-IMPACT program revealed the real diagnosis: histiocytic sarcoma, a blood cancer so rare it affects roughly 300 Americans a year, driven by an activating **MAP2K1 (MEK1)** mutation. Given roughly two months to live on standard therapy, Wolff was treated off-label by oncologist Dr. Mrinal Gounder with **trametinib**, a MEK inhibitor developed and approved for melanoma — not blood cancer. His tumor mass dropped **80% within ten days**; he has now been cancer-free for roughly a decade and returned to performing. This case clears an unusually high evidentiary bar: it was published as a formal case report in the *New England Journal of Medicine* and independently corroborated by CBS News, Newsweek, and MSK's own press office, giving the team four independent citations for one story ([NEJM 2018](https://www.nejm.org/doi/full/10.1056/NEJMc1511490); [CBS News 2025](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/cancer-genetic-sequencing-research-histiocytic-sarcoma-michael-wolff/); [MSK News](https://www.mskcc.org/news/how-msk-precision-diagnostics-saved-musician-with-rare); [Newsweek 2025](https://www.newsweek.com/2025/01/24/cancer-cures-genetic-sequencing-update-studies-scientists-2013421.html)).

The second anchor case, **Dr. Lukas Wartman**, is a leukemia researcher at Washington University in St. Louis who was himself diagnosed with acute lymphoblastic leukemia. After he relapsed in 2011 and DNA sequencing of his tumor found no actionable target, his colleagues turned to **RNA sequencing** and discovered the gene **FLT3 was massively overexpressed** — a target for **sunitinib**, a kidney-cancer drug never before given for adult leukemia. He became the first person treated with it for ALL; his disease went into complete remission. This story ran as the opening installment of the *New York Times*' three-part "Genetic Gamble" investigative series in 2012, independently corroborated by his home institution's own press office ([NYT, Gina Kolata, 2012](https://www.nytimes.com/2012/07/08/health/in-gene-sequencing-treatment-for-leukemia-glimpses-of-the-future.html); [Washington University School of Medicine](https://medicine.washu.edu/news/treatment-for-doctor-with-leukemia-points-to-personalized-medicine/)). Both cases share a narrative template — standard treatment fails or misdiagnosis occurs, sequencing finds an unexpected target, a repurposed drug produces a dramatic and quantifiable turnaround — that maps directly onto a "hope restored" beat.

For breadth beyond these two headline cases, **Cancer Commons**, a patient-advocacy nonprofit that says it has guided over 10,000 patients, publishes a library of real (first-name, de-identified) patient stories. Three are independently confirmed with clinical detail: **Rachel**, 52, with triple-negative breast cancer, achieved a complete pathologic response after profiling matched her to a trial combination therapy ([Cancer Commons](https://cancercommons.org/patients/rachel-breast)); **Susan**, 72, with stage IV NSCLC, was saved from an *incorrect* EGFR-targeted drug when genomic review found her actual driver was an ERBB2 alteration — a useful "mismatch avoided" variant on the theme ([Cancer Commons](https://cancercommons.org/patients/susan-lung)); and **Elyse**, 55, with metastatic endometrial cancer, achieved sequential partial responses across five-plus therapy lines as her treatment was re-matched to shifting biomarkers ([Cancer Commons index](https://cancercommons.org/patient-stories/page/2/)). These should be labeled to the judging panel as "patient advocacy testimonials," not peer-reviewed case reports, since exact mutations and drugs are often withheld from the public-facing story — but they demonstrate that Wolff and Wartman are not isolated flukes. Notably, no single named, fully detailed patient narrative was found published directly by the NCI-MATCH, TAPUR, or MD Anderson IMPACT trial programs themselves — these programs report rigorous cohort statistics (see below) but their individual patient stories, if they exist in trial-site testimonial pages, were not located in this research pass and remain a gap worth a targeted follow-up search of `cancerwise.mdanderson.org` and NCI's own patient-story features.

## The 70% figure has no home — but the real numbers are stronger

The most defensible replacement statistic for the team's unverified "70% of cases" claim is the **Schwaederle et al. meta-analysis** (JCO, 2015), which pooled 570 phase II single-agent trials and 32,149 patients published between 2010 and 2012: personalized, biomarker-matched treatment strategies produced a median response rate of **31% versus 10.5%** for non-personalized strategies, and median progression-free survival of **5.9 versus 2.7 months** ([JCO 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4737863/)). This is large, peer-reviewed, and purpose-built for exactly the "matched beats unmatched" comparison the pitch needs — it should be the team's headline data slide.

It is corroborated by several independent cohorts. The **MD Anderson IMPACT registry**, in its long-term follow-up of 3,487 screened patients, found that among the 1,307 patients with an actionable alteration, those who received matched targeted therapy had an objective response rate of **16.4% versus 5.4%** for non-matched therapy, disease control of **35.3% versus 20.3%**, and — critically for a causal claim — matched therapy remained an independent predictor of longer overall survival on multivariate analysis (hazard ratio **0.72**, p<0.001), with 10-year overall survival of **6% versus 1%** ([J Hematol Oncol 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6937824/)). A caveat the team should acknowledge proactively: IMPACT is a non-randomized registry, not a randomized trial, so "associated with" is the honest framing, not "proven to cause" — and IMPACT's own randomized follow-up, IMPACT2, did not cleanly replicate the survival benefit, per commentary describing it as raising "challenges and opportunities" rather than confirming the effect ([PMC 2022](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9612600/)). A more recent (2024) systematic review of real-world molecular tumor board data across 12,176 patients and 26 cancer types found similar direction: matched therapy carried hazard ratios of **0.46 for overall survival** and **0.65 for progression-free survival**, both favoring molecularly tailored treatment ([npj Precision Oncology 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11965449/)). A small (n=44) single-institution retrospective study additionally found matched therapy produced longer median survival (51.7 vs. 25.8 weeks) *with lower* weekly healthcare costs ($2,720 vs. $3,453) — useful as an illustrative cost-savings data point, but small enough that it should be flagged as supportive rather than definitive ([Oncotarget 2018](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC5844748/)).

The **NCI-MATCH** and **TAPUR** trials add texture but not a clean matched-vs-unmatched comparison, since both are single-arm registries by design. NCI-MATCH screened 5,954 patients: an actionable alteration was found in **37.6%**, but only **17.8%** were ultimately assigned to a matched treatment arm — a citable statistic in its own right about the gap between genomic potential and clinical delivery ([JCO 2020](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33048619/)). TAPUR reports results cohort-by-cohort; **21 of 34 published cohorts (61.8%)** met their pre-specified bar for a positive treatment signal, with response rates ranging from a dismal 3.7% (palbociclib in sarcoma) up to 25% (pertuzumab-trastuzumab in HER2+ colorectal cancer) — a useful caution that "molecularly matched" does not always mean "highly effective," which the team can pre-empt as evidence of intellectual honesty ([Targeted Oncology](https://www.targetedonc.com/view/tapur-results-roll-out-bringing-potential-new-paradigms-into-focus); [ASCO Post](https://ascopost.com/news/january-2020/data-from-colorectal-cancer-cohorts-of-tapur-study-presented/)). One credible outside skeptic worth naming if judges push back: Vinay Prasad has estimated only **5-9%** of cancer patients benefit from genome-driven therapy, when the denominator is restricted to FDA-labeled genome-driven drugs alone ([JAMA Oncology 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29710180/)) — a view directly disputed by precision-oncology researchers as overly restrictive because it excludes the off-label, trial-based matching that IMPACT, NCI-MATCH, and TAPUR all study ([Nature commentary](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28550030/)). Acknowledging this debate rather than hiding it will make the pitch more credible to a skeptical panel, not less.

## The testing gap is Sarah's real threat, not genomic pessimism

If the storyboard needs a statistic-driven "near miss" for Sarah rather than a single named tragic case, the strongest peer-reviewed anchor is a 2026 JAMA Network Open study of **63,294 patients** with advanced breast, prostate, colorectal, pancreatic, and lung cancers drawn from the Flatiron Health database: for most cancer types **only about half of patients received genomic sequencing at all**, and median time to testing ranged from under one month in advanced NSCLC to **11 months** in metastatic prostate cancer, with lower socioeconomic status independently associated with longer delays ([JAMA Network Open 2026](https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.5585)). STAT News' contemporaneous coverage crystallizes the headline: **over 40%** of these 63,000-plus patients received no genomic testing whatsoever ([STAT News 2026](https://www.statnews.com/2026/04/07/genomic-tests-needed-for-targeted-cancer-treatments/)). This pattern repeats across tumor types: in NSCLC, only **67%** of all patients and **77%** of nonsquamous metastatic patients received ALK/EGFR/PD-L1 testing within 90 days of diagnosis ([AJMC](https://www.ajmc.com/view/real-world-insights-on-biomarker-testing-patterns-and-implications-for-mnsclc-therapy-selection)); despite having insurance, **61.8%** of metastatic NSCLC patients missed NCCN-concordant biomarker testing within that same window ([OncLive](https://www.onclive.com/view/gaps-and-future-directions-in-nsclc-biomarker-testing-liquid-biopsy-mrd-and-non-actionable-alterations)); and in colorectal cancer, RAS testing was frequently ordered only *after* first-line treatment had already begun ([JNCI Cancer Spectrum](https://academic.oup.com/jncics/article/6/6/pkac065/6712321)).

Timing matters independently of whether testing happens at all. A 2024 JCO Precision Oncology study of 2,694 advanced NSCLC patients found that when comprehensive genomic profiling results arrived *before* first-line therapy started, matched targeted-therapy use rose by **14 percentage points (17% vs. 2.8%)**, and driver-positive patients whose testing was timely had a significantly longer time to treatment discontinuation (adjusted hazard ratio **0.50**, p=2.0×10⁻⁶) plus an estimated **$13,659 per-patient reduction** in costs from ineffective immunotherapy given on the wrong assumption ([JCO PO 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10939592/)). The honest caveat here: this same study found *no* statistically significant overall survival difference (p=0.12) — the team should claim "faster to the right drug, and cheaper," not "proven to extend survival." A separate retrospective cohort of 92 NSCLC patients re-biopsied at disease progression found **85% (74/87)** had at least one actionable alteration revealed at that later point, roughly a third of which were not present or found at initial diagnosis — yet only **5%** of those patients actually went on to receive matched treatment, mostly because their health had declined too far by the time testing caught up ([Cancers (Basel) 2021, PMC8749927](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8749927/)). This is the sharpest available "the match existed but arrived too late" statistic in the research, and it maps directly onto a "the answer was there, but too late" beat.

For a single dramatized individual case of harm from delay, the most citable is a September 2026 KFF Health News/CBS News investigation into **Mason Henderson**, a 21-year-old diagnosed with a rare brain cancer (diffuse hemispheric glioma) whose neuro-oncologists identified a biologically plausible off-label PARP-inhibitor option after standard treatment failed; insurance and a pharmacy benefit manager denied coverage twice, and by the time his mother's public campaign secured compassionate access, he received the drug for only two months before dying ([CBS News 2026](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/rare-cancer-drugs-insurance/)). This case must be framed carefully: it is an access/insurance-delay story about an *off-label, biologically-rationalized* match, not a case where an approved on-label drug existed and testing simply wasn't ordered — the outlet itself does not claim the drug would have saved him if given sooner. Used honestly, it dramatizes the "paperwork versus a ticking clock" tension rather than a false claim about a guaranteed cure denied. Disparities compound the delay problem: LUNGevity's advocacy data states that **Medicaid patients are 40% less likely** to get biomarker testing than privately insured patients, and **30% less likely** to receive a targeted therapy even after testing occurs ([LUNGevity](https://www.lungevity.org/research/patient-focused-research-center-patient-force/barriers-to-optimal-care/barriers-to)) — useful as a supporting equity statistic, though it should be labeled as advocacy-sourced rather than peer-reviewed.

## Fragmented data is Dr. Alina's 2am problem, evidenced not anecdotal

The literature does not offer a single named, first-person oncologist quote decrying the difficulty of manually synthesizing multi-omics data — this was searched for directly and not found, and the team should not fabricate or paraphrase one as a verbatim quote. What it offers instead is something arguably stronger for a judging panel: converging, independently-run peer-reviewed surveys. A structured Mayo Clinic-led study testing physicians on variant-of-uncertain-significance case vignettes found only **13 of 84 physicians (14.6%)** gave the literature-supported correct answer across all three test cases, and nearly half incorrectly defined what a VUS even is ([Familial Cancer 2018](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29721668/)). A 2018 industry survey of more than 160 oncologists found **nearly 60%** cited difficulty interpreting test results as a key reason they don't order genomic tests more often, citing the sheer number of different tests and conflicting results across vendors ([STAT News 2018](https://www.statnews.com/2018/06/20/genomic-testing-barriers-precision-medicine/)). An Australian nationwide survey of 108 medical oncologists found confidence dropped from a median of 70 (discussing standard CGP results) to just **56 out of 100** when discussing secondary germline findings, and **62.9% (68 of 108)** said they needed or wanted assistance interpreting genomic reports ([Oncotarget 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8522847/)). The pattern holds even in pediatric oncology, where only **35%** of physicians were confident interpreting somatic genomic results and just **27%** for germline results ([Cancer 2017](https://acsjournals.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/cncr.30581)). Confidence scales inversely with data complexity — worst for whole-genome/whole-exome sequencing, best for single-gene tests — which is precisely the "fragmented multi-omics" scenario the storyboard dramatizes; the pain is greatest exactly where an integrated tool would add the most value.

The time cost of manual synthesis is also measurable. Before digital tumor-board tooling, average case-preparation time ran **65 minutes** for a breast cancer tumor board, **42.6 minutes** for GI, and **52.4 minutes** for ENT cancer boards — and this is preparation time alone, not the independent chart review an individual oncologist does beforehand ([JCO Clinical Cancer Informatics 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7469605/)). International guidance recommends no more than 14 days elapse between a molecular result and multidisciplinary discussion, itself an implicit admission that weeks routinely pass without dedicated infrastructure ([Journal of Thoracic Oncology 2025](https://www.jto.org/article/S1556-0864(25)00831-7/fulltext)). The field has its own vocabulary for this: multiple independent 2023-2025 papers describe a "data tsunami" or "data deluge," explicitly stating that genomic data volume has outpaced clinicians' practical capacity to interpret it manually ([Clinical and Experimental Medicine 2025](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10238-025-01965-9); [Molecular Cytogenetics 2024](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13039-024-00698-w)) — a phrase literally usable as an on-screen title card since it originates from the field itself, not from the team's own dramatization. Underneath all of this sits a documented workforce gap: the Association for Molecular Pathology only published its first "Body of Knowledge" standard for clinical bioinformaticians in 2024, explicitly because no established competency framework previously existed for the specialists oncologists are supposed to lean on ([PMC 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13169022/)) — evidence that Dr. Alina is often working without backup, not evidence of personal failure.

## Conclusion

The team's storyboard does not need to invent drama — it needs to stop hedging with an unsourced statistic when better, harder numbers and two extraordinarily well-documented individual patients are sitting in the literature. The Wolff and Wartman cases are strong enough, and independently verified enough, to carry the "hope restored" beat without embellishment; the Schwaederle meta-analysis is strong enough to carry the credibility slide without inflation. The more interesting finding for the team's own thinking, though, is that the *testing-gap and interpretation-burden* evidence is actually more solid and more consistent across independent studies than the "matched therapy works" evidence — the latter is real but comes mostly from non-randomized registries with an honestly-reported randomized trial (IMPACT2) that didn't fully confirm it, while the former (40%+ untested, 61.8-77% missing testing windows, 14.6% VUS accuracy, 62.9% wanting help) is corroborated across half a dozen independently-run studies from different institutions and years. That asymmetry argues for a storyboard that leans harder on "the system is too fragmented and too slow to use what it already knows" than on "genomic matching is a guaranteed miracle" — the former is the argument a GNN multi-omics tool actually solves, and it happens to be the argument with the sturdiest evidence behind it.
