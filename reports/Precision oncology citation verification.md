# Four of five deck citations hold, with fixes

All five citations on the "Problem Statement" and "Why This Project Exists" slides trace to **real, non-fabricated papers** — none is a hallucinated DOI or a phantom journal. But three carry errors that a fact-checking judge would catch: the JAMA Network Open/STAT News slide's **"41 days" NSCLC figure is not in the source and should be deleted or replaced** with the paper's actual 0.96-month (~29-day) median; the **"data tsunami" citation has the wrong title and the wrong paper for the clinician-overload framing** — the Hsu et al. 2025 paper is actually titled "data **deluge**," and the phrase "data tsunami" belongs to a different, separately real paper (Aldea et al., *ESMO Open*, 2023), which is also the better source for the overwhelmed-clinician framing; and the **Macklin et al. VUS-interpretation figure (13/84) is quoted correctly from the abstract but the abstract's own arithmetic is wrong** — 13/84 computes to 15.5%, not the 14.6% printed in the paper. The fourth citation, the Schwaederle et al. 2015 *JCO* meta-analysis, is fully correct as used and needs no changes. Below is the verdict for each, followed by exactly what to fix and the working link to cite.

## The JAMA/STAT testing-gap pairing is sound, but "41 days" must go

The anchor citation for Sarah's near-miss is solid at its core. DOI `10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2026.5585` resolves cleanly to a real, actively-discussed article — Hage Chehade et al., "Trends and Disparities in the Use of Next-Generation Sequencing in Patients With Cancer in the United States," *JAMA Netw Open.* 2026;9(4):e265585, a 63,294-patient Flatiron Health EHR cohort across five advanced/metastatic cancers, independently corroborated by Targeted Oncology, AJMC, Medscape, Healio, and Managed Healthcare Executive, all describing the identical study and cohort size ([JAMA Network Open](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2847397)). A correction issued May 7, 2026 softened the abstract's "most patients" to "many patients," which is itself a useful signal: the paper does not print a single "over 40%" headline sentence, but a weighted average of its five per-cancer-type non-testing rates (33.0% colorectal to 59.7% metastatic breast) comes to approximately **41.3%** of the full cohort — a figure independently reproduced by Medscape's own coverage, so "over 40%" is defensible as a derived, corroborated statistic rather than a verbatim quote ([Medscape](https://www.medscape.com/viewarticle/tumor-genomic-testing-too-little-too-late-2026a1000b7g)). The companion STAT News piece is also real and correctly dated — Angus Chen, "Many cancer patients don't get genomic tests to guide treatment, study finds," April 7, 2026 — though its visible (non-paywalled) text says "roughly half" received sequencing, not an explicit "over 40%" figure; the exact STAT wording sits behind a STAT+ paywall and should not be quoted verbatim without confirming it first ([STAT News](https://www.statnews.com/2026/04/07/genomic-tests-needed-for-targeted-cancer-treatments/)).

**The one figure that must come off the slide is "41 days."** The paper reports median time to NGS testing for advanced NSCLC as **0.96 months (95% CI, 0.93–0.96)** — about 29 days, not 41 — and metastatic prostate cancer as the slowest at **11.0 months (95% CI, 9.8–11.0)**, exactly matching the "~11.0 months" half of the claimed comparison. So the directional claim (NSCLC fastest, prostate slowest at ~11 months) is correct and should stay; only the specific "41 days" figure is unsupported by this paper, and no other source checked in this verification contains that number either — it most likely came from a transcription error, a different statistic entirely (e.g., a mean rather than median), or conflation with an uncited source. The safe, citable replacement is "median time to NGS for NSCLC was under one month (0.96 months) versus 11.0 months for metastatic prostate cancer" — using the paper's own units rather than a converted day-count it never states.

## "Data tsunami" is the wrong title pointing at the wrong paper's framing

This citation checks out as a real paper, but with two compounding errors that both need fixing. First, the title itself is wrong: the Clinical and Experimental Medicine 2025 paper the team is citing is titled **"AI-driven multi-omics integration in precision oncology: bridging the data deluge to clinical decisions"** — "deluge," not "tsunami" — by Hsu CY et al., *Clin Exp Med* 2025;26(1), DOI `10.1007/s10238-025-01965-9`, independently confirmed via Europe PMC's metadata mirror and an open-access PMC copy ([PMC12634751](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12634751/); DOI link [doi.org/10.1007/s10238-025-01965-9](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10238-025-01965-9)). Second, and more consequential for the slide's argument, **this paper does not support the "clinicians overwhelmed by information" framing at all.** An end-to-end full-text search for "overload," "cognitive burden," and "complexity of decision-making" returned zero matches anywhere in the article; its "data deluge" language is explicitly about computational and statistical burden (dimensionality exceeding sample size, >10 TB of raw data per patient needing storage and compute), not about an individual oncologist's cognitive load ([Europe PMC full text](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC12634751/fullTextXML)).

**The actual source of the phrase "data tsunami" is a different, separately real paper that is also the better fit for the clinician-overload framing:** Aldea et al., "Precision medicine in the era of multi-omics: can the data tsunami guide rational treatment decision?", *ESMO Open* 2023;8(5):101642, DOI `10.1016/j.esmoop.2023.101642`, independently verified via Europe PMC and an open-access PMC copy ([PMC10539962](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10539962/)). The phrase appears only in this paper's title, not its body — but its Section 6.3 contains language genuinely close to the overload framing the slide wants: "The scientists and clinicians are however dealing with huge amount of data that cannot be integrated by a single person," in a passage about bioinformatics reaching its limits for clinical decision support. Neither paper contains the exact sentence "clinicians may face an overload of information, increasing the complexity of decision-making" verbatim, so if that precise wording is needed as a quotation, it should be rephrased as a paraphrase attributed to Aldea et al., not presented as a direct quote from either source. The cleanest fix: cite Hsu et al. 2025 only for the general "multi-omics data volume and heterogeneity requires AI-driven integration" point (with the title corrected to "data deluge"), and cite Aldea et al. 2023 separately for the literal phrase "data tsunami" and the clinician-overload framing.

## Macklin's VUS figures are quoted right — the paper's own abstract did the math wrong

The Macklin et al. paper is real, correctly titled, correctly authored, and the PMID the team already has (29721668) is confirmed as the right record via an independent DOI-to-PMID lookup ([NCBI E-utilities](https://eutils.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/entrez/eutils/efetch.fcgi?db=pubmed&id=29721668&rettype=medline&retmode=text)). One date correction: the formal journal issue is **January 2019** (*Fam Cancer* 18(1):121–126), not 2018 — 2018 is only the epub/PubMed-indexing year, so if the deck wants to keep "2018" it should read "2018 (Epub); Fam Cancer 2019" rather than plain "2018," which a reviewer could flag as mismatched against the official issue date. The working link is `https://doi.org/10.1007/s10689-018-0086-2` or `https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29721668/`.

**All three headline statistics the deck cites are printed essentially verbatim in the paper's own abstract**, confirmed independently through both Europe PMC's `abstractText` field and NCBI's MEDLINE record: "Only 13/84 (14.6%) responded to all three case examples with the answer deemed 'most correct' by review of literature"; "Around half (39/84) of the physicians incorrectly defined a variant of uncertain significance"; and "Over 75% made a recommendation for genetic testing that was not warranted" ([Europe PMC](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/search?query=DOI:10.1007/s10689-018-0086-2&format=json&resultType=core)). **But the first figure contains an arithmetic error that originates in the published paper itself, not in how the deck is citing it: 13/84 computes to 15.5%, not 14.6%.** No erratum or correction notice for this discrepancy was found in this search. This is not a misattribution — the sentence is genuinely in this paper's abstract — but a judge who does the division will get a different number than the slide states. The safest options are to either reproduce "13/84 (14.6%)" exactly as the paper states it with a footnote flagging the apparent rounding inconsistency in the source, or restate it honestly as "13 of 84 physicians (~15%)" and drop the paper's own 14.6% figure. Either way, the discrepancy should be disclosed rather than silently inherited.

## Schwaederle's meta-analysis is clean and ready to present

Of the five, this citation needs no correction. The statistics cited — **31% vs. 10.5% response rate, 5.9 vs. 2.7 months progression-free survival, 13.7 vs. 8.9 months overall survival, all P < .001**, across **570 phase II single-agent trials and 32,149 patients** published January 2010–December 2012 — all belong unambiguously to Schwaederle M, Zhao M, Lee JJ, et al., "Impact of Precision Medicine in Diverse Cancers: A Meta-Analysis of Phase II Clinical Trials," *Journal of Clinical Oncology* 2015;33(32):3817–3825, DOI `10.1200/JCO.2015.61.5997`, PMID 26304871, PMCID PMC4737863 — confirmed by both Crossref and Europe PMC, which agree exactly on every field ([Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1200/JCO.2015.61.5997); [PMC4737863](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4737863/)). The one thing worth flagging only as a point of care, not a correction: there is a separate, easily-confused 2016 *JAMA Oncology* paper by the same lead author (Schwaederle et al., DOI `10.1001/jamaoncol.2016.2129`) covering phase I trials with different numbers (30.6% vs. 4.9% response rate, no OS reported) — the deck's title, journal, and all four statistics match the 2015 *JCO* phase II paper specifically, not that one, so no change is needed, but anyone double-checking later should be careful not to swap in the wrong sister paper. "Nearly tripling" (31%/10.5% ≈ 2.95x) is a fair paraphrase of the abstract's own language, not a quoted phrase from the paper.

## What this means for the deck

Three of the four problems are fixable in minutes: delete "41 days" and use the paper's own "under one month" framing, retitle the data-tsunami citation to "data deluge" while moving the overload framing to Aldea et al. 2023, and either footnote or correct the Macklin 13/84 percentage. None of the five citations is fabricated, and the single most important slide for the credibility argument — the Schwaederle meta-analysis carrying the "matched therapy works" data point — needs no changes at all. The pattern worth noting for future fact-checking passes: every error found here originated either in how a figure was transcribed onto the slide (the 41-day number, the tsunami/deluge title swap) or in the source paper's own internal inconsistency (Macklin's abstract math) rather than in a fabricated or mismatched reference — which means the fix is editorial, not a search for new sources.
